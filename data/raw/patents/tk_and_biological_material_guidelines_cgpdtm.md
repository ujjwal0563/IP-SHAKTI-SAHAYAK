# Guidelines for Processing of Patent Applications Relating to Traditional Knowledge and Biological Material
**Official Code:** CGPDTM Guidelines 2012 / Circular No. 1 of 2012  
**Authority:** Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs & Trademarks (CGPDTM)  
**Jurisdiction:** IN  
**Category:** PATENT  

---

### Section 1 - Mandate and International Framework for Traditional Knowledge Protection
It has been reported that the Indian Patent Office is granting patents on the use of traditional knowledge (TK) of India, particularly relating to the Ayurveda, Unani and Siddha systems of medicine, and patents have been granted on inventions related to biological resources obtained from India without taking adequate care to observe the mandate of law.
India has played a pivotal role in bringing the protection of traditional knowledge to the centre stage of the International Intellectual Property System (WIPO Inter-Governmental Committee on IP and Genetic Resources, Doha Ministerial Declaration 2001, and UN Convention on Biological Diversity - CBD).
India concluded TKDL Access (Non-Disclosure) Agreements with major international patent offices (USPTO, EPO, JPO) resulting in cancellation, withdrawal, or amendment of numerous patent applications appropriating Indian traditional knowledge.

---

### Section 2 - Statutory Provisions under the Indian Patents Act, 1970
Traditional knowledge, by its very definition, is in the public domain and hence, any application for patent relating to TK does not qualify as an invention under Section 2(1)(j) of the Patents Act, 1970 ("new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application").
Furthermore, statutory patent bars under Section 3 strictly apply:
- **Section 3(e):** A substance obtained by a mere admixture resulting only in the aggregation of the properties of the components thereof or process for producing such substances is not an invention.
- **Section 3(p):** An invention which, in effect, is traditional knowledge or which is an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known component or components is strictly non-patentable.
- Sections 3(b), 3(c), 3(d), 3(f), 3(h), 3(i), and 3(j) are also of direct relevance to patent applications related to TK and biological materials.

---

### Section 3 - Biological Diversity Act Mandates & NBA Approval
Section 6(1) of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 provides that no person shall apply for any intellectual property right, in or outside India, for any invention based on any research or information on a biological resource obtained from India without obtaining the previous approval of the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA).
- **Mandatory Declaration:** In Form-1 (Application for Grant of Patent) under paragraph 9, the applicant must declare whether the invention uses biological material from India and that necessary permission from NBA shall be submitted prior to grant.
- **Statutory Grounds for Refusal/Revocation:** Contravention of law constitutes grounds for refusal under Section 15, pre-grant opposition under Section 25(1)(d), (f), (k), and post-grant revocation under Section 25(2)(d), (f), (k) of the Patents Act, 1970.
- Non-disclosure or wrongful disclosure of source/geographical origin of biological material is a ground for opposition and revocation under Section 25(1)(j) and 25(2)(j).
- Under Section 55(1) of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, contravention of Section 6 is punishable with imprisonment or fine up to 10 lakh rupees or commensurate with damage.

### Guiding Principle 1 - Extracts and Active Ingredients Pre-known in Traditional Knowledge
If the subject-matter as claimed relates to extracts/alkaloids and/or isolation of active ingredients of plants, which are naturally/inherently present in plants, such claims cannot be considered as novel and/or inventive when the use of such plants is pre-known as part of the teachings of Traditional Knowledge.
- When claims relate to extracts containing undefined active ingredients, they lack novelty if the therapeutic use of the plant is pre-known in TK.
- If claims relate to isolated and characterized active principles/alkaloids not forming part of prior art, they lack an inventive step because the prior art motivates a person skilled in the art to isolate active ingredients responsible for known therapeutic effects.
*Illustration 1:* Claims to an extract of *Withania somnifera* (Ashwagandha) for stress management are anticipated and lack novelty over TKDL references disclosing *Withania somnifera* roots for stress-related disorders.
*Illustration 2:* Claims to an isolated alkaloid (Chamaemeloside) from German chamomile for wound healing lack an inventive step over TKDL citations disclosing chamomile's therapeutic use for wounds and skin disorders.

---

### Guiding Principle 2 - Obvious Combinations of Plants with Known Therapeutic Effect
Combination of plants with known therapeutic effect with further plants with the same known therapeutic agents wherein all plants are previously known for treating the same disease is considered to be an obvious combination (mere additive effect).
- It is normally expected that combining medicinal plants with known individual effects will produce an additive therapeutic effect without involving an inventive step.
*Illustration 1:* Composition comprising *Calendula officinalis*, *Aloe vera*, and *Centella asiatica* for wound healing is obvious over independent TKDL uses of each plant for healing.
*Illustration 2:* Composition of Ginger, Radish, Celery, and Black seed for male fertility is an obvious combination of individually documented aphrodisiacs in Ayurveda/Unani.

---

### Guiding Principle 3 - Presumption of Obviousness for Active Ingredients Known for a Disease
In case an ingredient is already known for the treatment of a disease, it creates a presumption of obviousness that a combination product comprising this known active ingredient would be effective for the treatment of the same disease.
- Inventive merit cannot be acknowledged unless the applicant demonstrates a surprising, unexpected, or superior synergistic effect of the combination compared to the known single ingredient.
*Illustration:* A five-constituent formulation containing watery extract of *Cucumis melo* for vitiligo is presumed obvious over Unani references disclosing *Cucumis melo* anti-vitiligo properties, in the absence of demonstrated synergy.

---

### Guiding Principle 4 - Discovering Optimum or Workable Ranges by Routine Experimentation
Discovering the optimum or workable ranges of traditionally known ingredients by routine experimentation is not inventive.
- Selecting a specific percentage or ratio of known herbal ingredients falls well within the expectations of a person skilled in the art.
*Illustration:* A topical formulation comprising specific percentages of *Pongamia pinnata* (Karanj 2-20%), *Lawsonia alba* (Heena 5-15%), and *Cocos nucifera* oil (20-60%) for ulcers/wounds is obvious over traditional Agasthiyar formulations, as optimizing ratios is routine experimentation.

---

### Guiding Principle 5 - Isolation of a Single Component from Multi-Ingredient Traditional Formulations
In case multiple ingredients are known to have the same therapeutic activity as per traditional knowledge, taking out one single component out of them cannot be considered as inventive.
*Illustration:* Extract of *Zingiber zerumbet* (bitter ginger) for asthma is obvious over multi-component anti-asthmatic Unani formulations containing bitter ginger.

---

### Guiding Principle 6 - Combining Known Traditional Ingredients with Other Plant Ingredients
In case individual ingredients are already known for the treatment of a disease as a part of Traditional Knowledge, it is obvious that a combination product comprising these known ingredients with further plants with the same known therapeutic effect would be more effective than each applied separately (additive effect).
*Illustration:* Composition of theanine from tea combined with Shankhapushpi (*Convolvulus pluricaulis*) or Shatavari (*Asparagus racemosus*) for cold and immunity is obvious over known immunomodulatory and adaptogenic references in Ayurvedic classical literature.

---

### Section 4 - Exemption Clarification for Commodity Trade vs Medicinal Use
Circular No. 1 of 2012 by CGPDTM clarifies:
The exemption to medicinal plants from the provisions of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 under Ministry of Environment and Forests Notification dated 26th October 2009 is available **only if they are traded as commodities**.
The provisions of the Biological Diversity Act are strictly applicable if biological resources are used as ingredients for medicine or in patent inventions. Approval of the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) is mandatory for any biological resource derived from India and used in an invention.
