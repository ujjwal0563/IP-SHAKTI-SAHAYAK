# Botanical Drug Development: Guidance for Industry

**Authority:** U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)
**Center:** Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER)
**Publication Date:** December 2016
**Jurisdiction:** US
**Category:** REGULATORY

## 1. Introduction
This guidance provides recommendations to sponsors on the development of botanical drugs intended to be submitted in New Drug Applications (NDAs) or Over-the-Counter (OTC) drug monographs. A botanical product may be a food (including a dietary supplement), a drug (including a biological drug), a medical device, or a cosmetic. 

If a botanical product is intended for use in diagnosing, curing, mitigating, treating, or preventing disease in humans, it is regulated as a **drug** under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act).

## 2. Botanical Drugs vs. Dietary Supplements (DSHEA)
It is critical to distinguish between Botanical Drugs and Dietary Supplements.
- **Dietary Supplements:** Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), a botanical product can be marketed as a dietary supplement. It may bear "structure/function" claims (e.g., "helps maintain a healthy cardiovascular system"), but it **cannot** bear "disease" claims (e.g., "treats heart disease"). It does not require FDA premarket approval for efficacy.
- **Botanical Drugs:** If a sponsor wishes to make a disease claim, the product must undergo the Investigational New Drug (IND) process and receive approval via a New Drug Application (NDA).

## 3. General Principles of Botanical Drug Development
Because botanical drugs are complex and heterogeneous mixtures, the FDA recognizes that identifying the active ingredients and ensuring consistent batch-to-batch manufacturing is difficult.
- **Active Ingredient Identification:** Unlike purified chemical drugs, it is often not possible to identify the active ingredients in a botanical drug.
- **Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC):** The FDA emphasizes clinically relevant specifications, multiple chemical fingerprints (e.g., HPLC, mass spectrometry), and strict control of raw botanical materials (Good Agricultural and Collection Practices - GACP).
- **Prior Human Experience:** The FDA acknowledges that many botanical products have a history of traditional use (e.g., Ayurvedic and Traditional Chinese Medicine). Extensive prior human experience can often substitute for some conventional preclinical animal toxicology testing, allowing sponsors to proceed safely into Phase 1 or Phase 2 clinical trials more rapidly.

## 4. Late-Phase Clinical Development
To receive NDA approval, botanical drugs must meet the same statutory standards for safety and substantial evidence of effectiveness as non-botanical drugs.
- Due to the natural variability of botanical raw materials, the FDA requires a "totality of the evidence" approach to ensure therapeutic consistency.
- Synergistic effects of multiple compounds within a botanical extract are recognized, which is why purification to a single compound is not strictly required.
